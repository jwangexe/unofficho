A buffer holds the pH roughly steady when small amounts of acid or base are added. It does this with a weak acid HA and its conjugate base A⁻, both present in comparable amounts: A⁻ mops up added H⁺, and HA mops up added OH⁻.

## Where the equation comes from

Start from the acid dissociation constant and take $-\log_{10}$ of both sides:

$$
K_\mathrm{a} = \frac{[\mathrm{H^+}][\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a} + \log_{10}\frac{[\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]}
$$

This is the <F id="henderson-hasselbalch" />. So far it is exact. The approximation comes when you substitute the amounts you **added** for the equilibrium concentrations. That only works when HA and A⁻ are both much more concentrated than H⁺ and OH⁻.

Because HA and A⁻ share the same volume, you can use **moles** in the ratio, and dilution does not change the pH.

## Worked example: a titration

25.0 mL of 0.100 mol L⁻¹ ethanoic acid ($\mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a} = 4.76$) is titrated with 0.100 mol L⁻¹ NaOH.

- At **12.50 mL**, half the acid has been neutralised, so $n(\mathrm{HA}) = n(\mathrm{A^-})$ and $\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a} = 4.76$. This is the half-equivalence point.
- At **20.00 mL**, there is 2.00 mmol of A⁻ and 0.50 mmol of HA left, so $\mathrm{pH} = 4.76 + \log_{10} 4 = 5.36$.

<Callout type="trap">
When strong acid or base is added to a buffer, do the **stoichiometry first**, as a complete reaction with A⁻ or HA. Only then put the new amounts into Henderson–Hasselbalch. Putting the added H⁺ straight into the log term is the most common error.
</Callout>

## How good is the buffer?

Buffer capacity $\beta$ is the amount of strong base needed per litre to raise the pH by one unit. Ignoring water's own contribution, for total concentration $C = [\mathrm{HA}] + [\mathrm{A^-}]$ and fraction deprotonated $\alpha$:

$$
\beta = \ln 10 \cdot C\,\alpha(1-\alpha)
$$

This is largest when $\alpha = \tfrac12$, that is when pH = pKₐ. It falls to about a third of that maximum at pKₐ ± 1. That is where the rule "choose an acid with pKₐ within one unit of the target pH" comes from.

<Callout type="tip">
A higher total concentration gives more capacity at the same pH. A buffer of 0.01 mol L⁻¹ ethanoic acid with 0.01 mol L⁻¹ ethanoate has the same pH as one at 1 mol L⁻¹, but only a hundredth of the resistance.
</Callout>
