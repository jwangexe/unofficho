Many mechanisms pass through a reactive intermediate that never builds up. Take the simplest case:

$$
\mathrm{A} \xrightleftharpoons[\;k_{-1}\;]{\;k_1\;} \mathrm{I} \xrightarrow{\;k_2\;} \mathrm{P}
$$

The exact rate equations are coupled, so we approximate. There are two standard approximations. The pre-equilibrium approximation is a special case of the steady-state one, so knowing which you are using, and why, is most of the work.

## The steady-state approximation

If I is consumed about as fast as it is made, its concentration stays small and nearly constant after a short induction period. Set its net rate of change to zero:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{I}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = k_1[\mathrm{A}] - k_{-1}[\mathrm{I}] - k_2[\mathrm{I}] \approx 0
$$

so

$$
[\mathrm{I}] = \frac{k_1[\mathrm{A}]}{k_{-1} + k_2}
$$

The rate of forming P is then

$$
\text{rate} = k_2[\mathrm{I}] = \frac{k_1k_2}{k_{-1}+k_2}\,[\mathrm{A}]
$$

This is the steady-state result. Notice what it does **not** assume: nothing about which step is faster.

## The pre-equilibrium approximation

Now suppose I falls back to A much faster than it goes on to P, so $k_{-1} \gg k_2$. Then A and I stay close to equilibrium, with $[\mathrm{I}] = K_1[\mathrm{A}]$ where $K_1 = k_1/k_{-1}$, and

$$
\text{rate} = k_2K_1[\mathrm{A}] = \frac{k_1k_2}{k_{-1}}[\mathrm{A}]
$$

This is exactly the steady-state result with $k_2$ dropped from the denominator. The pre-equilibrium form is a limit, not a rival theory.

The opposite limit, $k_2 \gg k_{-1}$, gives rate $= k_1[\mathrm{A}]$. Almost every I that forms goes on to P, so the first step controls the rate.

<Callout type="trap">
Pre-equilibrium needs $k_{-1} \gg k_2$. It does **not** need $k_1 \gg k_2$. A slow forward step and a fast back step can still be in equilibrium. Look at what competes for I: the back reaction against the forward one.
</Callout>

## Using the approximation in exam problems

- Apply the steady-state condition to **intermediates only**, never to reactants or products.
- With two intermediates, write one steady-state equation for each and solve them together.
- Check the answer against the limits. Does it collapse to something sensible when one rate constant dominates?
