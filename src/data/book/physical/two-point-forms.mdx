Questions write these equations in several different-looking ways. They are all the same equation, and each comes from one line of algebra. Once you can derive it, you never need to remember which way round the fractions go.

## Arrhenius: from one temperature to two

Start from the <F id="arrhenius">Arrhenius equation</F> and take natural logs:

$$
k = A\,e^{-E_\mathrm{a}/RT} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln k = \ln A - \frac{E_\mathrm{a}}{RT}
$$

Write it at two temperatures, $T_1$ and $T_2$:

$$
\ln k_1 = \ln A - \frac{E_\mathrm{a}}{RT_1} \qquad \ln k_2 = \ln A - \frac{E_\mathrm{a}}{RT_2}
$$

Subtract the first from the second. $\ln A$ cancels, and $\ln k_2 - \ln k_1 = \ln(k_2/k_1)$:

$$
\ln\frac{k_2}{k_1} = -\frac{E_\mathrm{a}}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_2}-\frac{1}{T_1}\right)
$$

That is the <F id="arrhenius-two-point">two-point form</F>. The only assumption is that $A$ and $E_\mathrm{a}$ do not change between the two temperatures.

## van ’t Hoff: the same algebra for K

Here the starting point is thermodynamics, not kinetics. Two expressions for $\Delta G^\circ$ (see <F id="gibbs-k" />):

$$
\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K \qquad\text{and}\qquad \Delta G^\circ = \Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ
$$

Set them equal and divide by $-RT$:

$$
\ln K = -\frac{\Delta H^\circ}{RT} + \frac{\Delta S^\circ}{R}
$$

This has exactly the shape of $\ln k = \ln A - E_\mathrm{a}/RT$. Write it at $T_1$ and $T_2$ and subtract; $\Delta S^\circ/R$ cancels just as $\ln A$ did:

$$
\ln\frac{K_2}{K_1} = -\frac{\Delta H^\circ}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_2}-\frac{1}{T_1}\right)
$$

That is the <F id="vant-hoff">van ’t Hoff equation</F>, assuming $\Delta H^\circ$ and $\Delta S^\circ$ are constant over the range.

<Callout type="note">
The A-level specification covers the Arrhenius half of this page. The van ’t Hoff half is for UKChO and beyond.
</Callout>

| | Arrhenius | van ’t Hoff |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Constant | rate constant $k$ | equilibrium constant $K$ |
| Energy term | $E_\mathrm{a}$, always positive | $\Delta H^\circ$, either sign |
| Cancels on subtracting | $\ln A$ | $\Delta S^\circ/R$ |
| Raising $T$ | always increases $k$ | increases $K$ only if endothermic |

## One equation, many looks

All of these say the same thing, written for $k$ (swap in $K$ and $\Delta H^\circ$ for van ’t Hoff):

| Form | What changed |
| --- | --- |
| $\ln\dfrac{k_2}{k_1} = -\dfrac{E_\mathrm{a}}{R}\left(\dfrac{1}{T_2}-\dfrac{1}{T_1}\right)$ | as derived |
| $\ln\dfrac{k_2}{k_1} = \dfrac{E_\mathrm{a}}{R}\left(\dfrac{1}{T_1}-\dfrac{1}{T_2}\right)$ | minus sign used to swap the bracket |
| $\ln\dfrac{k_1}{k_2} = \dfrac{E_\mathrm{a}}{R}\left(\dfrac{1}{T_2}-\dfrac{1}{T_1}\right)$ | minus sign used to flip the ratio |
| $\ln\dfrac{k_2}{k_1} = \dfrac{E_\mathrm{a}(T_2-T_1)}{RT_1T_2}$ | bracket over a common denominator |
| $\log_{10}\dfrac{k_2}{k_1} = \dfrac{E_\mathrm{a}}{2.303\,R}\left(\dfrac{1}{T_1}-\dfrac{1}{T_2}\right)$ | base-10 logs: $\ln x = 2.303\log_{10}x$ |

<Callout type="tip">
Check the sign instead of memorising it. If $T_2 > T_1$, the rate must go up, so $\ln(k_2/k_1)$ must come out positive. If your arrangement gives a negative number, a minus sign has gone astray.
</Callout>

## Worked example

A rate constant doubles between 298 K and 308 K. Find $E_\mathrm{a}$.

$$
\ln 2 = \frac{E_\mathrm{a}}{8.314}\left(\frac{1}{298}-\frac{1}{308}\right) = \frac{E_\mathrm{a}}{8.314}\times 1.090\times10^{-4}
$$

So $E_\mathrm{a} = 0.693 \times 8.314 / (1.090\times10^{-4}) \approx 5.29\times10^{4}$ J mol⁻¹, or 52.9 kJ mol⁻¹. This is the origin of the old rule of thumb that rates double for every 10 K rise.

<Callout type="trap">
Three slips cause most wrong answers. $R$ is in J, so $E_\mathrm{a}$ or $\Delta H^\circ$ must be in J mol⁻¹, not kJ. Temperatures must be in kelvin. And the bracket is a difference of reciprocals, $1/T_1 - 1/T_2$, not $1/(T_2 - T_1)$.
</Callout>
