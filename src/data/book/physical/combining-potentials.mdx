Standard electrode potentials look like they should add up like enthalpies, but they don't. A potential is an energy **per electron**, which makes it intensive. Gibbs energies are extensive, and they are the quantities that add.

## The rule

For each half-reaction, convert to Gibbs energy with <F id="gibbs-emf" />:

$$
\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ
$$

Add the $\Delta G^\circ$ values along any path, then convert back using the electron count of the overall half-reaction:

$$
E^\circ_\text{overall} = \frac{\sum_i n_iE^\circ_i}{\sum_i n_i}
$$

## Worked example: iron

Find $E^\circ(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}/Fe})$ from the two tabulated couples:

| Couple | $n$ | $E^\circ$ / V | $nE^\circ$ / V |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mathrm{Fe^{3+} + e^- \to Fe^{2+}}$ | 1 | +0.77 | +0.77 |
| $\mathrm{Fe^{2+} + 2e^- \to Fe}$ | 2 | −0.44 | −0.88 |
| $\mathrm{Fe^{3+} + 3e^- \to Fe}$ | 3 | ? | −0.11 |

So $E^\circ = -0.11/3 \approx -0.04$ V. That is nowhere near the plain sum (+0.33 V) or the average (+0.17 V).

<Callout type="trap">
Multiplying a half-equation by 2 doubles $\Delta G^\circ$ but leaves $E^\circ$ unchanged. If you double a potential to balance a cell, you have mixed up the two quantities.
</Callout>

## Full cells are the exception

When two half-reactions make a balanced cell, the electrons cancel and both halves carry the same $n$. That $n$ divides out, which is why the <F id="ecell">cell potential</F>, $E^\circ_\text{cell} = E^\circ_\text{cathode} - E^\circ_\text{anode}$, works with raw potentials.

## Latimer diagrams and disproportionation

A Latimer diagram lists one element's oxidation states left to right, from most to least oxidised, with $E^\circ$ for each step. A species in the middle **disproportionates** if the potential on its right is higher than the one on its left. That means it is a better oxidant than its higher oxidation state is.

Copper is the classic case. Cu²⁺ —(+0.15 V)→ Cu⁺ —(+0.52 V)→ Cu, so aqueous Cu⁺ falls apart into Cu²⁺ and copper metal.

<Callout type="tip">
To skip a state in a Latimer diagram, use the weighted average above. Comproportionation is the reverse test. If the potential to the right of a middle species is **lower** than the one to its left, the two outer species react to give it.
</Callout>

These are standard potentials. For real concentrations, correct each couple with the <F id="nernst">Nernst equation</F> before comparing.
