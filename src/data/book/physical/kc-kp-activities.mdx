You will meet three equilibrium constants. At A-level, $K_\mathrm{c}$ and $K_\mathrm{p}$ carry units. At university and in olympiads, $K$ has no units, because it goes inside a logarithm in $\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$. They are the same idea, written with different conventions.

## Kc: concentrations, with units

For $a\mathrm{A} + b\mathrm{B} \rightleftharpoons c\mathrm{C} + d\mathrm{D}$, the <F id="kc">concentration equilibrium constant</F> uses equilibrium concentrations:

$$
K_\mathrm{c} = \frac{[\mathrm{C}]^c[\mathrm{D}]^d}{[\mathrm{A}]^a[\mathrm{B}]^b}
$$

Its units are $(\text{mol dm}^{-3})^{\Delta n}$, where $\Delta n = (c + d) - (a + b)$. For $\mathrm{N_2} + 3\mathrm{H_2} \rightleftharpoons 2\mathrm{NH_3}$, $\Delta n = -2$, so $K_\mathrm{c}$ is in mol⁻² dm⁶. When $\Delta n = 0$ the units cancel, and so does the volume.

## Kp: partial pressures, with units

For gases, use partial pressures instead. The <F id="partial-pressure">partial pressure</F> of a gas is its mole fraction times the total pressure, $p_\mathrm{A} = x_\mathrm{A}\,p_\text{total}$, and the partial pressures add up to the total. Then

$$
K_\mathrm{p} = \frac{p_\mathrm{C}^{\,c}\,p_\mathrm{D}^{\,d}}{p_\mathrm{A}^{\,a}\,p_\mathrm{B}^{\,b}}
$$

with units of $(\text{pressure})^{\Delta n}$. The number depends on the unit chosen: for $\Delta n = -2$, $K_\mathrm{p}$ in Pa⁻² is $10^6$ times smaller than in kPa⁻². Only gases appear in $K_\mathrm{p}$.

## From Kc to Kp

For an ideal gas, $p = \frac{n}{V}RT = cRT$, so every partial pressure is its concentration times $RT$. Substituting into $K_\mathrm{p}$, each factor of $RT$ appears once per mole of gas:

$$
K_\mathrm{p} = K_\mathrm{c}\,(RT)^{\Delta n}
$$

Use SI units throughout: concentrations in mol m⁻³ and pressures in Pa (so $R = 8.314$ J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ links them directly).

## The unitless K: activities

The thermodynamic $K$ replaces each concentration or pressure with an **activity**, a pure number that says how far a substance is from its standard state:

| Substance | Activity (ideal) | Standard state |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solute | $c/c^\circ$ | $c^\circ = 1$ mol dm⁻³ |
| Gas | $p/p^\circ$ | $p^\circ = 1$ bar = 100 kPa |
| Pure solid or liquid, solvent | 1 | the pure substance |

So $K = \prod_i a_i^{\nu_i}$ has no units, and $\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$ makes sense: you can only take the logarithm of a pure number. For real, non-ideal mixtures each activity also carries an activity coefficient, $a = \gamma c/c^\circ$; olympiad problems almost always take $\gamma = 1$.

Numerically, $K$ equals $K_\mathrm{c}$ with concentrations in mol dm⁻³, and $K$ equals $K_\mathrm{p}$ with pressures in bar. Dividing the ideal-gas link by the standard states gives the unitless version of the conversion, the <F id="kp-kc" /> relation:

$$
K_p = K_c\left(\frac{c^\circ RT}{p^\circ}\right)^{\Delta n}
$$

<Callout type="trap">
Putting a $K_\mathrm{p}$ in kPa straight into $\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$ gives the wrong answer unless $\Delta n = 0$. Convert the pressures to bar first, i.e. divide each by $p^\circ$ = 100 kPa.
</Callout>

## Which one to use

| | $K_\mathrm{c}$ | $K_\mathrm{p}$ | unitless $K$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| From | concentrations | pressures | activities |
| Units | $(\text{mol dm}^{-3})^{\Delta n}$ | $(\text{kPa})^{\Delta n}$ | none |
| Solids | left out | left out | activity 1 |
| Used in | A-level | A-level | university, olympiads |

<Callout type="tip">
In an A-level exam, give $K_\mathrm{c}$ and $K_\mathrm{p}$ with units. In an olympiad, if a question links $K$ to $\Delta G^\circ$ or $E^\circ$, treat it as unitless and make sure every concentration is in mol dm⁻³ and every pressure in bar.
</Callout>
